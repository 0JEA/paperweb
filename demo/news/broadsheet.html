Council Votes to Daylight
the Buried River

After forty years underground, the Wexford will be opened from the rail yards to the harbour, a decision taken at 1.40 a.m. by a single vote.

By Anneke Ruthven
Staff correspondent · Reporting from City Hall

The vote came at twenty minutes to two in the morning, after the public gallery had thinned to a dozen people in raincoats and the clerk had twice been asked to read the amendment back. When it carried, seven to six, nobody cheered. A woman near the door put her hand flat against the wall as though checking it for a pulse.

For four decades the Wexford has run in a concrete culvert beneath Harrow Street, audible through the grates after heavy rain and otherwise forgotten by everyone except the crews who inspect it. Under the plan approved Friday, eleven hundred metres of that culvert will be opened to the sky, beginning at the disused rail yards and ending where the river already surfaces at the harbour wall.

A cost nobody disputes

Engineers put the work at $184 million over six years, a figure neither side contested. What they contested was the sequence: whether the flood channel is widened before the street grid is rebuilt, or after.

“You cannot phase a river,” said Councillor Miriam Osei, who introduced the amendment and who has argued for the project since her first term. “It is going to arrive whether the paperwork is ready or not.”

Opponents on the finance committee said the estimate omits the cost of relocating two substations and a fibre trunk that follows the culvert for its entire length. The city engineer conceded the substations are not in the figure.

Where the water goes

Hydrologists retained by the province modelled a hundred-year storm against the opened channel and found peak levels a metre below the new embankment, provided the two upstream weirs are removed on schedule.

Removal of the weirs requires a federal permit that has been pending since March. Officials in Ottawa said Friday only that the file is “in the ordinary course.”

Residents of the Harrow Street terraces, whose back gardens end at the culvert wall, will be offered a right of first refusal on the widened easement. Eleven of the fourteen households have already written to the clerk in support.

Forty Years in a Pipe

The Wexford was buried in 1986 to make room for a road that was never built. The road is still not built.

By Peter Oyelaran · Photographs by Ilse Marchetti

Meridian City — The culvert is four metres across at its mouth and smells of cold stone. Inspection crews descend twice a year, in pairs, tethered, and they have been doing it since the winter the concrete went in.

06 Op-ed and pull quote

Opinion

We buried it because we could not look at it

By Miriam Osei · Councillor, Ward 6

Nobody in 1986 thought they were destroying a river. They thought they were solving a smell. The Wexford took the runoff from three tanneries and a rail yard, and by August you could find it with your eyes shut from two streets away. Burying it was hygiene, and hygiene was the whole municipal religion of that decade.

The tanneries closed in 1994. The rail yard shut in 2003. The smell went with them, and what remained under Harrow Street was simply a river in a box, doing what rivers do, invisibly, at a cost of $2.1 million a year in inspection and grouting.

The argument against opening it has never really been about money. It is about the discomfort of admitting that a previous council, full of serious people acting on the best advice available, was wrong. I have sat in that chamber for nine years. I know exactly how much that costs.

There is a version of this city where the water is a fact you walk past on the way to work. We are eleven hundred metres from it.

They thought they were solving a smell. The smell left in 1994. The river stayed in the box.
Miriam Osei

Ms Osei has represented Ward 6 since 2017 and chairs the standing committee on water. A reply from the finance committee will appear Tuesday.

09 Obituary, black-ruled

Hester Lindqvist

1931–2026

Hester Lindqvist, who as city hydrographer from 1968 to 1991 measured the Wexford every week of its final free-flowing decade and then, under protest, signed the survey that authorised its burial, died on Tuesday at her home in Northgate. She was ninety-four.

She kept the field books. All twenty-three of them are in the municipal archive, in her hand, in pencil, with the water temperature noted to a tenth of a degree and, in the margin for 14 September 1986, the single word enough.

She is survived by a daughter, two grandsons, and a river.

A service will be held Saturday at eleven o'clock at St Brannock's, Northgate. In lieu of flowers, the family asks for donations to the river survey fund.
